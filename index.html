01 — Research Development

What is a research developer?

A research developer has the skills of a data analyst and a software engineer — and does the human work in between.

Most technical work needs two different people: someone to find and make sense of the information, and someone to build the thing. Then it needs a third person to talk to everybody and figure out what was actually wanted in the first place. I do all three.

That matters most when a problem arrives messy, which is how real problems always arrive. "We'd like software that does this." "We want to be able to model this." "We have all this data and we're not sure what to do with it." None of those are specifications yet. Turning them into one is the first part of the job.

How a project actually goes

  1. 1

    We talk it through

    You describe what you need in plain language — no jargon required, and no need to have it figured out yet. I ask questions, talk to the people who'll use it, and turn all of that into a clear plan we both agree on before anything gets built.

  2. 2

    I go find the information

    Whatever it takes to get the data: searching online, reading through the scientific literature, calling and emailing people who might know, digging through filing cabinets and digitizing paper records, or writing scrapers to collect it automatically. I'll do the legwork, in person or otherwise.

  3. 3

    I design it

    How the information should be organized, how the pieces fit together, and what the thing should look like to use. This is the part that decides whether it still works in three years, so it's worth doing carefully.

  4. 4

    I build it

    The database, the data pipeline, the software, the model, the simulation, the game — whichever of those your problem calls for. Built properly, so it can be maintained and added to later.

  5. 5

    We put it in front of real people

    The people who'll actually use it sit down with it while I watch. Then I fix what confused them. This step gets skipped constantly, and skipping it is why so much software is miserable to use.

  6. 6

    I hand it over properly

    Packaged, documented, and published or installed wherever it needs to live. You get something you own and can keep using — not something that only works while I'm around. If it needs to become a paper, I can write that too.

Why this is affordable now

AI has changed what it takes to build software. Things that once required a substantial amount of engineering time — writing boilerplate, building data pipelines, testing ideas, getting a prototype running — can now happen much faster.

That makes custom software useful to a much wider range of people. A professor doesn't need a huge grant to explore an idea. A small business doesn't need a full-time developer to build a tool that would save them hours every week. A community organization can build something that fits its particular needs rather than adapting to whatever software already exists.

The interesting part, to me, is figuring out what should actually be built. That's still a human problem: understanding the research or business question, figuring out what the data can tell us, choosing an approach, and making sure the result is actually useful.

The tools have gotten better. That means more interesting things are now possible.

Case study · University of Victoria · In progress

Fire Evacuation Modeling — a decision tool for the people who call the evacuation

When a wildfire moves toward a community, someone has to decide when to call the evacuation and which roads to send people down. That decision is currently made with experience, maps, and very little time. I'm building a simulation that lets planners rehearse it beforehand.

It models two things at once: how the fire spreads across real terrain, and how traffic moves while it does. You can close a road and watch the evacuation re-route around it, dispatch a fire truck through closures, or branch the whole timeline to compare two decisions side by side. The interaction borrows from games — free-flying camera, click the terrain, branching saves — because that turns out to be a genuinely good way to explore "what if we'd done it differently".

The engineering choice I care most about: the simulation core is plain C# with no dependency on the game engine at all. That means it can be unit-tested, run headless in batches, and — critically — have peer-reviewed fire science swapped in behind its interfaces as the research matures, instead of being rewritten each time.
